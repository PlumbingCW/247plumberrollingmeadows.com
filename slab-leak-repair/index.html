Emergency Slab Leak Repair in Rolling Meadows, IL

A slab leak is a leak in a pipe that runs under or inside the concrete floor of a house. Warm spots on the floor, the sound of running water and an unexplained jump in the bill are the signs. Dispatch is answered 24/7, and if you suspect a slab leak, shut off the main water valve and call.

Why a Slab Leak Cannot Wait

Water escaping under a slab can undermine the soil beneath the concrete, soak flooring and feed mold. A hot-water line that leaks into the slab also wastes energy, since the heater keeps running to replace what is lost. The sooner the leak is found, the smaller the repair.

Close the main valve if you hear running water you cannot explain or find a hot spot. Then call 24/7. If water is coming up through a floor crack, keep electrical devices and people away from the wet area.

Signs of a Slab Leak

Warm or hot spot on the floor

A leaking hot-water line can heat the concrete above it.

Running water sound with all fixtures off

Water moving through a damaged pipe is audible in a quiet house.

Water bill jump or moving meter

Closed taps and a turning meter dial point to a leak.

Damp carpet, buckling flooring or baseboards

Moisture comes up through the slab and damages what sits on it.

Low water pressure

A leak on the supply side reduces pressure at fixtures.

Cracks in walls or floors

Persistent moisture can shift the soil and stress the foundation.

Does My House Have a Slab?

Not every home has plumbing in a slab. Many houses in the area have basements or crawlspaces, and their pipes run overhead or against the foundation. A slab leak applies to a house built on a concrete slab, or to a slab-on-grade addition such as a family room or garage.

If you are not sure, call and describe the house. The plumber will tell you whether the symptoms fit a slab leak or point to a leak elsewhere, and you can read about the broader search on the leak detection page.

Soil, Slabs and Age in Rolling Meadows

Rolling Meadows housing was built mostly between 1950 and 1980, according to the city's comprehensive plan, and later additions were often built on slabs. In older work the supply lines may be copper or galvanized, and copper in contact with concrete and soil can wear over decades.

We do not state a soil type or pipe condition for any home before we have seen it. The plumber checks the actual material and the age of the system, then recommends a repair or reroute based on what is under your floor. That keeps the advice about your house and not a generic guess.
