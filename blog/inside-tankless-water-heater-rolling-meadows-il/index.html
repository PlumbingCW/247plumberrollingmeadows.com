Inside Tankless Water Heaters for Rolling Meadows, IL Homes

An indoor tankless water heater frees floor space and heats water on demand, but it asks more of the house than a tank does. Venting, gas supply, condensate drainage and clearances all matter. This guide covers what to check before you commit.

By the Rolling Meadows 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated November 2026

    What Indoor Tankless Means

    Tankless heaters come in indoor and outdoor models. An indoor unit mounts on a wall inside the house, often in a basement, utility room or garage. It needs a vent to carry exhaust outside and a supply of combustion air, and it needs a gas line sized for a much higher flow than a tank heater uses.

    High-efficiency condensing models produce acidic condensate that must drain to a proper location, often with a neutralizer. Those requirements can change a simple swap into a larger installation, which is why a site visit comes first.

    Tank or Tankless

    A tankless unit can last longer and saves floor space, and it never runs out of hot water in the way a tank does, but it costs more to install and needs more from the house. Simultaneous heavy use can exceed its capacity if it is undersized.

    If your gas line, vent or space cannot support tankless without major work, a tank may be the better fit. Our tankless water heater page compares the two in more detail, and the water heater installation page covers planned swaps.

    Questions To Ask The Installer

    • How will the unit be vented, and with what material?
    • Does the gas line need to be upsized?
    • Where does the condensate drain?
    • What size unit do I need for my fixtures?
    • What is covered by the warranty and what maintenance is needed?

    Sizing For Your Household

    A tankless heater is rated by how many gallons per minute it can heat by a certain temperature rise. In winter, incoming water is colder, so the rise is larger and the flow rating falls. A unit sized for summer may disappoint in January.

    List the fixtures that could run at the same time, such as a shower and a dishwasher, and add their flow rates. The plumber uses that total, plus your coldest incoming water temperature, to pick a unit that will keep up.

    Maintenance And Lifespan

    Tankless heaters need periodic descaling, particularly in areas where scale builds up, plus cleaning of the inlet screen. Skipping maintenance can lower efficiency and cause error codes.

    With proper service, many units last longer than tank heaters, though parts and labor for repairs can cost more. Ask about the warranty on the heat exchanger and what voids it, since lack of maintenance sometimes does.

    Costs That Surprise People

    The unit price is only part of the cost. Upsizing the gas line, running new vent pipe, adding a condensate neutralizer and installing a recirculation line each add to it. Electrical outlets for controls and a service valve kit are also common extras.

    A written quote that lists each piece lets you compare installers fairly. If one quote omits vent or gas work, ask why before assuming it is cheaper, because those items are usually necessary.

    Noise, Placement And Daily Use

    Tankless units make a fan and burner sound while they run, so placement near bedrooms deserves thought. Many homeowners prefer a utility room, basement or garage wall. Leave space around the unit for service.

    Expect a short delay before hot water arrives, since the unit has to ignite and reach temperature. A recirculation pump or a small buffer tank can shorten the wait, and the plumber can explain the options.

    Ask whether the installer will register the warranty, set the temperature and walk you through the controls before leaving. A short orientation helps you understand error codes and the maintenance schedule, and it avoids a service call over a setting.

    Keep the installation manual, the permit paperwork and the commissioning notes together. They answer most questions a future plumber or buyer will ask, and they help prove the work was done to code if the house is ever sold.

    What Rolling Meadows Says About Venting and Permits

    The City of Rolling Meadows plumbing FAQ states that single-wall vent pipe requires 6 inches of clearance to combustibles and B-vent requires 1 inch. It also says a permit is required for a new or replacement water heater, with contractor licenses and bonds on file with Community Development.

    Many Rolling Meadows homes were built between 1950 and 1980, according to the city's 2018 plan, and older utility rooms may lack the space or air supply for a new unit. The plumber checks this at the site visit. The city is the source to confirm specific requirements.

    Palatine Differences

    We also serve Palatine, where the village has its own rules, such as combustion air openings for heaters in confined spaces and raising a gas heater in a garage. Read our Palatine page for a summary.

    Whichever town you live in, an indoor tankless installation should be planned, permitted and inspected. Ask for the permit details before you approve the price, and keep the paperwork for your records.
