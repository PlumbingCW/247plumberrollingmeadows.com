A Pipe Just Burst: Do This First

Close the main shutoff valve, usually near where the water line enters the house and the meter, by turning it clockwise. If you cannot find it, close the valve at the street only if you can safely reach it, and call the city's Public Works at 847-963-0500 on weekdays for help. Then open the lowest faucet and flush an upstairs toilet to drain the lines.

Switch off power to any wet room at the breaker if you can do so without stepping in water. Move valuables up, photograph the damage and call the 24/7 line. If the ceiling is bulging with water, keep clear of the area.

Why Pipes Burst in Northern Illinois

Water expands when it freezes, and the pressure builds in the section of pipe between the ice and a closed faucet. The pipe usually splits where the pressure peaks, which is often not where the ice formed. Pipes in exterior walls, crawlspaces, garages and unheated basements are most at risk during a cold snap.

Not every burst is a freeze. Corrosion in older galvanized pipe, high water pressure and a failed fitting can all cause a rupture. A plumber looks at the failure to decide whether it is a single defect or a sign that the pipe is near the end of its life.

Frozen Pipes That Have Not Burst Yet

If a pipe is frozen but not split, you may see no water at a tap while the rest of the house works. Open that tap, then warm the pipe slowly with a hair dryer or towels soaked in warm water, moving from the faucet back toward the frozen section. Never use an open flame.

If you see a crack or hear water once the pipe thaws, shut off the main right away. A thawed pipe often starts leaking where it split, and the water that was locked behind the ice is released all at once.

Copper, PEX and Galvanized Pipe

Copper splits along its length when it freezes and can sometimes be repaired with a coupling. PEX is more flexible and can survive freezing better, though fittings can still fail. Galvanized steel, which is common in older homes, corrodes from the inside and tends to fail at threaded joints, so a burst in an old galvanized line often means the rest is not far behind.

If your home was built between 1950 and 1980, which describes most of the housing in Rolling Meadows according to the city's comprehensive plan, ask the plumber what material the burst pipe is. That answer shapes whether a repair or a repipe is the sensible plan.

Dealing With the Water Afterward

Once the burst is repaired, drying matters as much as plumbing.

  • Remove standing water quickly and open windows or run fans to move air
  • Photograph all damage before you move anything, and keep damaged items for the adjuster
  • Call your insurer early, since many policies cover sudden pipe bursts but not long-term seepage
  • Watch for mold in damp drywall, carpet and cabinets over the next few days
  • Ask the plumber to document the cause on the invoice for your claim
