When Plumbing Is an Emergency

Call the 24/7 line right away if water is spraying or pouring from a pipe, sewage is coming up through a floor drain or tub, a ceiling is sagging with water, or you smell gas. For gas, leave the building first and call 911 or the gas utility before you call a plumber.

Some problems feel urgent but can safely wait for a same-day appointment when one is available. A slow kitchen drain, a dripping faucet and a toilet that runs but still flushes usually fall in that group. Dispatch will tell you which bucket your problem is in, and you decide from there.

As a rule of thumb, an emergency plumber visit is worth paying for when the water is either damaging the structure or making the house unsafe to use. Everything else can be scheduled.

What To Do Before the Plumber Arrives

A few minutes of action before we get there can keep a bad night from turning into a bad week.

Shut off the water

Find the main shutoff, usually where the water line enters the house near the meter, and turn it clockwise until it stops. Open a faucet at the lowest level to drain the lines.

Stop using drains

If sewage or gray water is backing up, stop running sinks, showers, washers and flushing toilets. Every gallon you add can end up on the floor.

Protect the electrics

Stay out of standing water near outlets, panels or appliances. If water reaches the panel, leave it alone and call the electric utility or 911.

Move what you can

Lift rugs, boxes and furniture off wet floors and photograph the damage. Your insurer will want to see what the water did before cleanup.

Once the water is under control, call (847) 281-8626 and tell dispatch what you found. A good description of the problem helps us send the right tools the first time.

Rolling Meadows Homes and What They Ask of a Plumber

Rolling Meadows was incorporated in 1955 after developer Kimball Hill began selling homes here in the early 1950s, and the city had about 700 houses sold by 1955. The city's 2018 comprehensive plan says most of its housing units were built between 1950 and 1980. For a homeowner that means a lot of houses with original-era water lines, drains and water heater closets, and plumbing that is now decades old.

Age is the reason we ask about pipe material on almost every leak and repipe call. Homes from that period may still carry galvanized steel supply pipe, which corrodes from the inside and chokes water pressure. A plumber can check the pipe where it enters the house and tell you in minutes whether to replace it now or monitor it.

Roughly a third of the city's housing is rented, according to that same plan, and many of the multifamily buildings stand three or four stories tall. If you manage a building or rent a unit, our commercial plumbing page explains how after-hours calls and shared drain lines are handled.

Know Who Owns What: City Water, Sewer and Your Pipes

The City of Rolling Meadows buys Lake Michigan water that is treated in Chicago and delivered through the Northwest Suburban Municipal Joint Action Water Agency. Lake Michigan has been the city's only regular source since 1986, with four deep wells kept as backup, according to its 2025 water quality report. Hardness and quality questions go to Public Works, so we send homeowners there for the annual report instead of quoting a number.

Ownership matters during a leak or a backup. On the water side, the city says it owns the service line from the main to the shutoff valve, and the homeowner owns the line from that valve into the house. On the sewer side, the homeowner owns the lateral from the building out to the city main.

For a sewer line problem, the city asks residents to call Public Works before calling a plumber, because a blockage in the main is the city's to clear.

A permit is required for a new or replacement water heater, per the city's own FAQ, so a water heater replacement here includes the permit step. Contractor licenses and bonds must be on file with Community Development before a permit is issued.
