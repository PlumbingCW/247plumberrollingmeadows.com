Tankless Water Heater in Rolling Meadows, IL

A tankless water heater heats water only when you open a tap, which saves floor space and ends the problem of a tank running empty. We install, repair and flush tankless units in Rolling Meadows. How a Tankless Heater Works

When you open a hot tap, a flow sensor starts a burner or electric elements, and water passes through a heat exchanger and leaves at the set temperature. When the tap closes, the unit shuts off. There is no stored tank, so there is no standby heat loss and no tank to fail and flood the floor.

The trade-off is capacity. A tankless unit has a limit on how many gallons per minute it can heat, so a household running two showers and a dishwasher needs a unit sized for that load, or a pair of units.

Flush and Descale Service

Minerals in water leave scale inside a tankless heat exchanger, which narrows the passages, reduces flow and can set off error codes. A flush uses a pump and a mild descaling solution to circulate through the unit and dissolve the buildup.

How often a unit needs it depends on the water and how much it is used. Rolling Meadows publishes no hardness value in its water quality report, so the best guide is the unit itself. If the output temperature fluctuates or flow drops, a flush is the first thing to try. Manufacturers also set their own service intervals, which the plumber can look up for your model.

Installing a Tankless Unit

Converting from a tank to tankless usually involves a larger gas line or new electrical capacity, a new vent path for gas models and a condensate drain for high-efficiency units. The plumber checks each before quoting, so the price reflects the whole job.

The City of Rolling Meadows requires a permit for a new or replacement water heater, and that includes tankless installs, per its FAQ. We take care of the permit with the city's Community Development Department. For the broader process, see water heater installation.

Hot Water That Matches Rolling Meadows Homes

Many homes here date from 1950 to 1980, per the city's comprehensive plan, and their gas lines and utility closets were sized for a tank. That does not rule out tankless, but it means the gas and venting review matters. A site visit beats a guess.

If the home has older galvanized supply pipe, low flow may hurt a tankless unit more than a tank. A plumber can check the supply pipe and pressure, and a repiping conversation may belong in the plan.
